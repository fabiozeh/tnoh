Why This Site, or: An Apology of Radically Simple Content Distribution

Fábio José · July, 2026

Majority-contradicting decisions — or simply "quirks" — naturally raise questions, or at least, eyebrows. I can imagine that, as soon as I share the first link from this domain, the recipients will begin asking themselves (or me directly), why not <insert blogging platform here>? This is obviously a deliberate choice, and one that I genuinely consider meaningful, so I'll try to explain my reasons and perhaps that reflection will make you a little more deliberate about how you choose to reach out to others, on- and offline.

Why not?

I should start by pointing out that, even though the plain old Home Page has fallen out of fashion, the majority of reasons for that have been technically and economically overcome. As I write this, in 2026, the open WWW standards are widely compatible across browsers and devices, much more powerful than they used to be — giving us "publishers" enormous freedom of form — and have also been made much, much more accessible, with quality documentation, learning tools and lots of dead simple libraries and code assistants, not to mention the possibility of using AI to get things just how you'd want without having to search for the mythical "Webmaster" in the Yellow Pages. To me, as a computer scientist, it's more than an overcome problem, it's a hobby in itself. I find it particularly fun to explore the limits of simple technology, and to demonstrate that we've had this stuff all along, we just needed to come up with the best ways to use it.

So I've made my case about the content itself, but what else do the alternatives bring to the table that I should address? Main things that come to mind are discoverability, analytics and social interaction. Maybe I'm in too deep in the UNIX philosophy, but I really do prefer tools to do "one thing well". I'd rather share and discuss this content on my social networks without necessarily tying the content itself to them. I find the separation refreshing. The absence of a comment box has benefits, too. You don't outsource your opinions before you even had a chance to form them, and you can channel the urge to interact in a more meaningful way. Send me an e-mail or something. Tell someone about this IRL.

Granted, I've mostly been grumpy and offered workarounds and barebones substitutions, so you may still be wondering what's the point of reinventing the wheel instead of using a blogging platform. Well, my reservations are not so much about what these platforms offer, but how. I am afraid I'll have to make the inevitable citation.

Marshall, I choose you!

Sadly, Mr. McLuhan is no longer around to prevent me misquoting him. For all his mid-century fame, I don't see enough comments these days about how the philosopher's mass media analysis applies to the digital world just as well, if not better. Therefore, when considering where to harbour my personal thoughts, one of my main concerns is how the choice of medium (with a small 'm') will inevitably bias the messages.

I think this is best explained with an example from a completely different thing. Drum machines. Yes, the electronic devices that play rhythmic sequences so you can create a beat for your music without a drummer or percussionist. These instruments are programmable, and very capable of producing all sorts of music, and yet, anyone that touches an Akai MPC-60 immediately sounds like a 90's rapper, and any song using a Linn Drum seems like a backing track for Prince. Similar effects happen with synthesizer models and even seemingly minuscule details like a guitar's strings gauges. Interfaces are like that; some things are easy to do, some things are hard, some things remind us of iconic references, and every detail nudges us in some direction. Before we know it, going against it feels as strange as talking slowly on TikTok.

I'm not saying that blogging in a personal Web Page makes me immune to that. I can feel the GeoCities ghosts creeping on me as I type. It does make me and you, reader, immune to shady EULAs and online tracking, but I can leave this banter for another day (or post). More to the point, though, choosing this primitive online space puts me conceptually closer to the people that inspire me the most.

The inspirations

Since you're Surfing the web right now, go and have a look at what some brilliant minds made available in it.

  • I've learned a good deal thanks to the books of Prof. Julius Smith that he made freely available in his personal home page. It also includes links to some of his band's music. Wild stuff.
  • Rodrigo Copetti decided to publish on his website everything he had learned about the computer architecture of video game consoles. It's incredibly rich and fascinating material. If you browse around you can also find the photos from his latest trips.
  • Fabrice Bellard, creator of FFMPEG among other things, hosts some really cool software projects that he's worked on for fun over the last three decades.

I think these incredible sites are the best possible explanation of why this is the medium I want to be in. Maybe I'll get to that level little by little. To wrap this up, my final recommendation is to check out the foundation from this very domain, SDF.org. They are a Seattle-based non-profit providing public access to remote computing for educational, cultural and entertainment purposes since 1987.

That's about it. I hope I've inspired you to make and share something cool, too.
